# Procedural planet terrain: research and implementation

This lab explores seeded, texture-free terrain on a planet. The references below
are primary research or documentation from the authors. Their algorithms are
research inputs, not a claim that the lab implements every result.

## Terrain formation and detail

- [A Review of Digital Terrain Modeling](https://diglib.eg.org/items/47f03175-46e8-44e8-94ef-663bef354d1c) (Galin et al., 2019) surveys procedural, simulation and example-based methods, and explains why homogeneous fractal noise alone produces weak landform structure. **Applied here:** distinct continental, mountain-belt and detail scales, with a land mask and warped 3D noise.
- [Generation of Folded Terrains from Simple Vector Maps](https://diglib.eg.org/items/5ecd72bf-b3a5-46be-b108-54e556b23f99) (Michel et al., 2015) explains the importance of connected mountain ranges and tectonic folds. **Applied here:** seeded spherical plate-center controls and a smooth boundary mask localize uplift. This is a procedural approximation; no plate motion is simulated.
- [Large Scale Terrain Generation from Tectonic Uplift and Fluvial Erosion](https://diglib.eg.org/items/13e52c36-0200-4652-aacf-17aa3098c5fd) (Cordonnier et al., 2016) combines uplift, stream networks and erosion. **Applied here:** a coarse, single-flow drainage graph and an artist-tuned stream-power-style erosion step. This is an independently written approximation, not their complete uplift and erosion model.
- [Physically-based Analytical Erosion for Fast Terrain Generation](https://diglib.eg.org/items/0a412929-2e29-4bcb-a597-1a4b2cc558e2) (Tzathas et al., 2024) combines stream-power solutions with hillslope and landslide processes. **Future research:** evaluate it as an optional cached world-generation stage, including its computational cost and license.
- [Real-time Terrain Enhancement with Controlled Procedural Patterns](https://diglib.eg.org/items/43319e3d-ba25-4762-971b-ac838ec21fb8) (Grenier et al., 2024) uses terrain slope to orient cascaded ravine patterns. **Applied here:** the general principle of slope-guided detail, using our own analytic value-noise gradients and directional filtering. This is not their phasor-noise algorithm; no paper code is included. The paper is published under a non-commercial license.
- [Terrain Amplification using Multi-Scale Erosion](https://h-schott.github.io/p/mserosion/) (Schott et al., 2024) combines erosion and deposition processes across scales to maintain hydrological structure. **Applied here:** cached coarse world drainage plus independently implemented, higher-resolution regional water and sediment simulation and procedural detail. The paper’s complete multi-scale erosion method is not implemented; fine detail is not hydrologically guaranteed.
- [Fast Hydraulic Erosion Simulation and Visualization on GPU](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/4392715/) (Mei, Decaudin and Hu, 2007) describes a GPU shallow-water approach to erosion and sediment transport. **Applied here:** the general idea of water-driven transport in a small independent regional solver, using our own four-neighbor flux and artist-tuned coefficients. This is not a reproduction of the paper’s solver.
- [FastFlow: GPU Acceleration of Flow and Depression Routing for Landscape Simulation](https://diglib.eg.org/items/5c046b92-a474-429d-9d0f-c028b55eef36) (Jain et al., 2024) motivates GPU flow accumulation and depression routing. **Applied here:** a separate, simpler fixed-iteration relaxation and gather pass; this is not FastFlow and does not inherit its convergence or performance guarantees.
- [r.watershed](https://grass.osgeo.org/grass85/manuals/r.watershed.html) documents single- and multiple-flow-direction watershed analysis. **Applied here:** one downhill neighbor per node on a depression-filled surface; unlike a full watershed model, the result is a coarse rendering approximation.
- [Generating Complex Procedural Terrains Using the GPU](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-i-geometry/chapter-1-generating-complex-procedural-terrains-using-gpu) (NVIDIA GPU Gems 3) describes multi-scale procedural fields and domain warping. **Applied here:** low-frequency 3D domain warp and bounded higher-frequency relief bands. The planet is a heightfield, not a volumetric cave system.

## Planet-scale rendering

- [Geometry Clipmaps](https://hhoppe.com/proj/geomclipmap/) (Losasso and Hoppe, 2004) and [GPU-based Geometry Clipmaps](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry) demonstrate nested grids and incremental GPU terrain updates. **Applied here:** reusable regular grids and GPU-side terrain generation; this lab uses a cube-sphere quadtree rather than a clipmap.
- [3D Tiles selection and screen-space error](https://cesium.com/learn/cesium-native/ref-doc/selection-algorithm-details.html) shows refinement based on estimated geometric error. **Current implementation:** measured patch residuals contribute to a projected-error priority, alongside a grid-cell-size floor and a hard patch budget. The residual estimates are not conservative bounds and do not guarantee a maximum screen-space error.
- [Horizon culling](https://cesium.com/blog/2013/04/25/horizon-culling/) discusses avoiding false culls when terrain crosses an idealized ellipsoid. **Applied here:** CPU-side conservative horizon rejection uses an inner and outer radius with a margin for bounded displacement.

## Implemented pipeline

1. Six consistently oriented cube faces form a sphere with an Earth-like mean radius of 6,371 km. A CPU quadtree selects patches within a user-controlled budget; levels can reach 20 when the camera and budget permit. Adjacent leaves are balanced to a maximum level difference of one, including across cube faces. Each generated patch is compared on the GPU against a grid with twice its cell spacing, including spherical curvature. The largest observed geometric residual is read back asynchronously; half that residual is used as a heuristic for the next finer level, and its priority influence is capped at 20% to limit patch churn. Unsampled patches use the projected grid-cell size; this floor and the patch budget remain in force. Previously split nodes receive a small priority margin to reduce churn. The residual is an estimate of finer-level error, not a conservative bound. This is spatial hysteresis, not a time-based terrain fade.
2. Each 16 × 16-cell patch has 16 meshlets of 4 × 4 cells, 32 triangles per meshlet. Coarse-neighbor edge flags collapse odd edge vertices to match the adjacent grid. New patches are generated and cached on the GPU; cached normals are updated when neighboring levels change.
3. Seeded plate centers, warped continental fields, ridged multifractals, directional folds and fine relief bands form the base terrain. Analytic gradients of larger ridges orient two scales of incised valley detail. Seeded bands displace vertices from tens of meters down to sub-meter scales. Four nested, world-space channel fields use interpolated coarse drainage direction, downhill slope and discharge to add drainage-oriented incisions, with high/low precision and frequencies chosen to keep shared edges stable. The directional influence fades near conflicting flow directions. These channels are an independently written procedural approximation. A separate regional hydraulic pass also modifies the terrain; there is no plate-motion simulation or photographic height map.
4. Once per seed, a separate GPU pass builds a world field on six 64 × 64 cube faces. The 24,578 vertices at their shared edges and corners use the same simulation data. On this coarse grid, fixed-iteration minimax relaxation approximates depression filling, one downhill neighbor is selected for each land node, and rainfall accumulates through the graph. Three passes use an artist-tuned stream-power-style rule to remove material and transport and deposit sediment. After routing, 128 gather iterations propagate the lowest bed elevation through each connected depression sharing a spill level; that floor supports a common bounded lake surface. The final graph, filled elevations, basin floors and erosion delta are cached. Changing the erosion slider scales that cached delta; it does not rerun the simulation for each slider position.
5. When the camera is within 50 km of the surface and **Regional water erosion** is enabled, a cached 3 × 3 neighborhood of fixed geographic tiles is generated on the GPU. Each tile spans about 50 km at a cube-face center and uses 64 × 64 cells (roughly 0.3–0.8 km spacing on the sphere), with a 14-cell halo. Six iterations route water through four neighbors, transport suspended sediment, erode where carrying capacity exceeds sediment load and deposit where it is exceeded. Initial water depends on the coarse rainfall and accumulated runoff. Rain, evaporation, carrying capacity and erosion/deposition caps are artist-tuned. The interior output records height change, suspended sediment and water; only height change modifies the visible mesh. Tiles are cached by seed in a bounded 32-slot cache, independent of rendering LOD. The erosion control scales the cached height change rather than re-running the solver.
6. Overlapping halos make same-face regional tile borders agree. A narrow spatial blend handles cube-face parameterization differences, and the outer edge of the cached area tapers where neighboring regions are unavailable. New or evicted tiles invalidate affected cached terrain patches and dependent normals. This is not time-based geomorphing; a cache update may still introduce a small discrete change.
7. GPU-generated river splines join the coarse drainage graph nodes, with seeded broad bends strongest on flatter terrain. A denser coverage field is cached per terrain patch for channel carving, while visible water is evaluated at each fragment's geographic direction, independent of the current patch or LOD. Land and sea use the same seeded terrain field at every LOD; conservative bounds avoid evaluating all terrain detail at unambiguous locations. A bounded, smooth reduction of ridge detail near sea level produces less fragmented coasts while preserving higher coastal relief. Ocean shading uses the continuous broad continental field as an inexpensive bed-depth approximation; shoreline edge filtering operates over a pixel footprint rather than changing the world-space coast. Narrow rivers use footprint-based filtering as they become subpixel. Lake coverage is localized around supported coarse filled depressions; a broader terrain field determines shorelines, and the water surface approaches a capped basin level. The mask comes from the seed's global field rather than the regional cache, so region loading does not create or remove lakes. To avoid spurious seas from interpolated erosion, already-dry land stays above sea level. Moisture- and temperature-inspired color uses runoff, rain, latitude and elevation; slope-dependent snow and highlight compression preserve highland contrast. These effects are analytical and use no image textures.
8. Compute passes calculate normals, approximate cavity shading and meshlet bounds. At a cube-face boundary, shared vertices use a consistent canonical normal, following coarser and same-level neighbor links at mixed-LOD corners; cached normals are refreshed when their dependency chain changes. A compute pass frustum-culls active meshlets and writes an indirect draw. A flat ocean uses spherical normals and no seabed cavity shadow; exposing the seabed restores terrain shading. Camera-relative positions, reversed-Z depth and high/low spherical patch centers improve large-world precision. Navigation searches and a small surface probe run on the GPU.

## Current limitations

- The world field has spacing on the order of 100 km, uses one flow direction per node and fixed iteration counts, and is not a calibrated physical simulation. Regional tiles are a six-step, four-neighbor approximation, with coarse runoff used as an initial water input; they do not exchange a full river-network flow boundary condition across the whole planet. Fine channels and the coarse river spline are not guaranteed to follow a monotonically descending regional terrain profile. Some seeds or more complex terrain could exceed the fixed iteration limits, including the connected-basin floor propagation. Regional water is evaporated and replenished by rainfall; erosion and deposition are capped for stability, and independently cropped and blended regions are not a globally mass-conserving watershed. Lakes are a filled-surface approximation; the displayed water is not a dynamic fluid surface.
- Climate colors are an artistic approximation, not a climate or ecosystem simulation. Rock materials, cast terrain shadows, clouds, atmospheric scattering and volumetric caves are absent; this is not photorealistic Earth.
- The quadtree combines a projected grid-cell-size floor with an asynchronously measured, projected terrain-residual priority. Its estimates are budget-limited, cover already generated patches and are not conservative geometric-error bounds. LOD hysteresis reduces unnecessary changes, but transitions remain discrete and can pop; edge stitching is not a proof of zero pixel seams. Water classification is world-based, but changes in the sampled surface geometry can still move its screen-space shoreline.
- Shared-edge GPU tests show sub-meter differences on coarse planet-scale patches on the test adapter; tests also exercise erosion-shaped land across a cube-face edge at close range and after camera movement. High/low sampling improves the smallest noise bands, but larger-scale fields and GPU implementation differences still limit precision; centimeter-scale guarantees need further work.
- Fine procedural bands are not fully frequency-filtered by projected sampling rate and can alias during descent. Stable band-limited residuals are future work.
- Patch selection runs on the CPU and is recomputed as the view changes. GPU geometry, river coverage and up to 32 regional simulation tiles are cached, but changing the seed requires world regeneration and camera jumps can create regional simulation and patch-generation bursts. Geographic land–sea classification and water shading evaluate terrain or river segments per fragment and can cost more than interpolating a patch mask. The regional lookup, output and working buffers add approximately 17 MB of GPU allocation. Real hardware should be profiled before making performance claims.
